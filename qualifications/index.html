What I was responsible for at Kittii

Product strategy and vision. Set the product vision, decided who it was for, and defined the main reason someone would use it. Built and maintained the roadmap from testing the idea through to a first working version. Judged which features to build, and in what order, against user value, what was practical to build, and business goals.

User research and discovery. Ran user interviews and conversations with customers to understand how people behave when they lose or find something, what frustrates them, and what they still need. Turned that feedback into product requirements and feature ideas, so the product was shaped around real problems rather than guesses.

Product design and user experience. Designed and improved the main journeys for both Seekers and Sherlocks. Produced wireframes, workflows and requirements so design and development stayed in step. Focused on removing hassle and building a simple experience people could trust.

Cross-functional collaboration. Worked with founders, developers, designers and others involved to turn business goals into product decisions, and led the discussions on scope, trade-offs, what to build first, and how to deliver it.

Testing and launch readiness. Developed the QA and user acceptance testing plans, established launch metrics and success criteria, and supported soft-launch planning and experimentation strategy.

What Kittii taught me

Kittii was my first experience of product management first hand. It taught me how to move from understanding user problems to designing solutions, how to balance competing priorities, and how to build in an environment where uncertainty is the norm.

It also confirmed that product is where I do my best work, at the intersection of people, business, technology and problem solving.

Across retail banking, lending, investments and financial advisory roles, I helped hundreds of Canadians navigate some of their most important financial decisions. Every week I met roughly 25 customers in one-on-one conversations to understand their goals, constraints and long-term plans. Rarely was the first problem presented the actual problem that needed solving. Success came from asking thoughtful questions, listening carefully, and uncovering the broader context behind a situation.

What the work covered

Lending. End-to-end applications for residential mortgages and home equity lines of credit, personal lending and credit analysis.

Investments and advice. Personalised financial advice across mutual funds, ETFs, GICs and savings solutions, plus small business banking and financing.

Retail support. Estate and trust account opening and maintenance, day-to-day banking, and creditor insurance conversations.

Team. Mentored and supported Personal Banking Associates and Tellers, developing their skills while working towards branch goals.

The four habits that carried into product

Customer discovery and empathy. Customers arrived asking for a specific product, but the real need sat beneath financial goals, fears, constraints or life events. Finding it required listening past what people said.

Root cause thinking. Many financial problems were symptoms. Whether the task was improving borrowing capacity or restructuring debt, the cause was often a spending pattern or an unreviewed statement balance. Addressing that, rather than simply submitting the application, is where I set myself apart.

Decisions under uncertainty. Every recommendation balanced incomplete information against customer goals, risk, policy and the available options.

Stakeholder management. Financial decisions involved customers, branch teams, credit adjudicators, specialists and business partners. Progress depended on bringing those views together towards a shared outcome.

Change Agent, Blink Foundation

Blink Foundation was an NGO focused on empowering school students and raising awareness of specific learning disabilities.

As a Change Agent I took part for two semesters, visiting two government-operated schools in Chennai. We worked with roughly 60 students per class and ran four to five sessions at each school, covering what a specific learning disability is, how students could recognise the signs, whom they could approach for support, and what resources were available to them.

I also joined a rural education camp in Karaikudi, working with rural school students over several days. Rather than relying on traditional teaching, we used stories, activities, role play and skits to make the learning practical: social values, kindness, empathy, handling pressure, and interacting respectfully within a community. Students acted out situations, created their own skits and demonstrated what they had learned.

The experience taught me that communication is not simply about delivering information. It is about understanding your audience and finding a way for the message to connect with them.
