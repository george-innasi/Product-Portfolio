How it started

The brief was to stop deconstructing products and start building one. Pick a product I use often, find one real friction, and design an AI-powered improvement I could actually prototype and demonstrate.

I kept landing on the same moment in my own week: an AI assistant hands me a clean draft, and I rewrite it anyway. The sentences are correct, but they are not mine, and they read the same whether I am writing to a customer or to my manager. That gap, between a polished draft and a sendable one, became the project.

Overview

Kural is an AI email assistant that drafts in your voice and reads the situation before it writes. It is not another model that polishes sentences; it classifies who you are writing to and why, holds a persistent model of how you sound, and produces a draft you can send without a rewrite. Built, shipped, and live at kural-iota.vercel.app.

📝 The Assignment

Design & prototype an AI-powered feature

You are the PM at a company whose product you use often. Identify one meaningful opportunity to improve it with AI, then design and prototype how that improvement would actually work. Focus on a single P0 problem, build a demonstrable prototype, and validate the thinking with PMs from that industry rather than inside the assignment.

⚪ The P0 problem

Even professionals who already use AI email tools rewrite the drafts they are given, because the tools polish sentences but do not read people: one voice, one register, every reader. 71% of AI users surveyed say they cannot send AI drafts as they are, and 22% have quietly abandoned the tools. The root cause is that generic assistants hold no persistent model of your voice and no structured read of the situation.

What the research said

A 36-person survey run through friends, WhatsApp and Reddit, plus four interviews. The question was simple: when you write an important email, where does the time and the friction actually go?

71%

of AI tool users cannot copy-paste the draft: it sounds like a robot and lacks their voice.

42%

say getting the tone right is the single hardest part of writing an important email.

75%

spend five minutes or more on a single important email, and many far longer.

What the numbers pointed to

  • Voice is the blocker, not grammar. The complaint was never that drafts were wrong. It was that they did not sound like the person sending them.
  • Tone is situational. The same person writes differently to a customer, a manager and a professor. Generic tools flatten all three into one register.
  • The work hides in wording, not typing. A third of respondents said their time goes into wording and tone, and a quarter into second-guessing what they already wrote.

Cross-checked against published research

A 36-person survey finds a signal; it does not prove one. So I held each finding against larger, published studies before writing it into the problem statement. The survey and the literature agreed.

Where they lined up

  • McKinsey puts the professional workweek spent on email at around 28%. The time cost is real and large.
  • Bynder found 62% of readers distrust copy that reads as bot-written, and 82% accept it once it feels human. The voice problem is a trust problem.
  • My own survey: 42% named tone as the hardest part and 75% spend five minutes or more per important email. The friction is specific and measurable.

Meet Kural

A composer, not an autopilot

You give Kural the gist in your own words. Before it writes a single line, it classifies the situation: how urgent it is, the power dynamic, and the emotional register. Then it drafts in your voice, shows you what it detected, and lets you adjust rather than regenerate from scratch.

The draft on the right is one pass. No "I hope this email finds you well." No padding. A message the sender could send as is.

How it works

Step 1Classify first
Step 2Adapt to the reader
Step 3Draft in your voice
Step 4Learn from your edits

The engine classifies before it composes: a situation object, carrying urgency, power dynamic and emotional category, is forced in the schema before any draft is generated. Situational rules with a defined precedence then shape the register, an anti-cliche guard strips the stock AI openers at both the prompt and the server, and the style memory learns how you sound from the edits you make. The result is a draft that reads the room and reads like you.

⚙ Under the hood

Deliberately simple, structured underneath

  • Classifies before it writes. A situation object is required in the schema before a draft exists, so tone is a decision, not an afterthought.
  • Adapts to who is reading. Writing upward, to a customer, or to a peer each pull a different register through rules with set precedence.
  • Learns your voice. Style memory updates from your edits, so the drafts drift toward how you actually write.
  • Never opens with a cliche. A prompt-plus-server guard removes "Hope you are doing well" and its relatives. Ever.

What is already out there

Capable tools exist. Each solves a nearby problem, and each leaves the voice-and-situation gap open.

The gap Kural takes

  • Superhuman is fast, but it asks you to replace your whole inbox to get there.
  • Ellie can train on your voice, but the voice-trained tier is the expensive one.
  • Gemini in Gmail is cheap and close at hand, but it stays generic unless you prompt it hard every time.
