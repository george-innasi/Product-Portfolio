How it started

Kittii began with friends from my post-graduate programme at Cape Breton University. The founder brought the idea to two of us, and it had already been put in front of Volta, the Atlantic Canada startup house, reaching the second round without getting through.

The feedback was that the idea was promising but the fundamentals were missing. That set the work for the next stage: do the discovery properly before building anything.

Overview

Kittii is an AI-powered lost-and-found marketplace. Seekers report what they have lost, a community of finders called Sherlocks report what they have found, and multimodal AI matching pairs the two.

The business problem is that recovery today is informal and slow. It depends entirely on whoever happens to be holding the item, and the person who lost it has no way to reach them.

Discovery

Why the incumbents left room

We ran discovery before committing to a build, rather than treating the idea as validated because it sounded good.

What did I find?

  • Demand is real and constant. People lose things, and the recovery path is informal and slow.
  • Almost every established platform was American and sold to institutions: airports, transit authorities, venues.
  • They catalogue what an organisation has found. None started from the person who lost the item.
  • None built a community of finders on the other side, which is the gap Kittii was designed around.
Competitor landscape

The Strategic Problem

Supply, not demand, is the constraint

Every competitor was supply constrained

The platform is built for Seekers, but it only works because of Sherlocks.

The chicken-and-egg problem is baked into a two-sided marketplace from day one. You cannot attract Seekers without Sherlocks reporting found items, and Sherlocks will not join a platform nobody has heard of.

  • Demand was never the bottleneck. Every lost-and-found platform I researched failed on the same constraint: not enough finders reporting.
  • Finders had no reason to report. The database stayed empty and Seekers left disappointed.
  • Timing compounds it. People who lose something need help inside a narrow panic window. Miss it and they have already given up.

So we flipped the model. Lead with Sherlocks, give finders a reason to report first, and let that supply base pull Seekers in.

The two-sided flow

Two journeys meet in the middle. A Seeker reports what they lost, a Sherlock reports what they found, AI pairs them, and the team validates ownership before any return or payment.

Go to Market

#foundatkittii, a five-day treasure hunt

One question shaped the whole plan: how do we get 100 or more people to actively report found items, in five days, in one city, for almost nothing?

How it worked

  • Ten partner shops across Halifax's downtown peninsula, each with a branded standee by the payment counter.
  • Each standee carried a unique clue tied to one item, plus a QR code to the submission form.
  • The first person to submit a matching report using that shop's clue won a $50 gift card, redeemable only at that shop.
  • Paid search intercepted "lost item Halifax" queries at peak intent, while Reddit and Instagram carried the campaign locally.

It turned ten business owners into acquisition channels for $50 each. No sales team, no cold outreach, and the shop's own foot traffic doing the distribution.

Binary thresholds, on purpose

The beta was capped at 200 to 250 submissions. A deliberate, controlled cohort rather than a push for scale.

Each number was set so the beta would return a clear go or no-go signal rather than feel-good engagement. A 30 per cent recovery rate is the one that matters, because it is the only measure of whether the product delivers end-to-end value.

This plan was never executed. The team wound down before launch, so the campaign did not run and none of these thresholds were tested.

What I would do differently

Two things I would change, both visible only in hindsight.

  • We set a revenue target before we had a repeatable way to acquire either side. A two-sided product has no value until both sides are present in the same place at the same time, and the plan treated supply and demand as sequential problems rather than one problem. The GTM eventually corrected for this, but it came late.
  • I would have tested the matching quality much earlier, against a small real corpus of lost and found reports. AI matching was the differentiator on paper, and it was the part with the least evidence behind it when the build started.

Where it stands. The build reached MVP and stopped there. The go-to-market plan was written but never run, and the revenue target was never tested. The product site stays up as a preview, though the product itself never launched.
