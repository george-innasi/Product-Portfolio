Problem statement

Frequent public transit commuters struggle to get lost belongings back, because finders have no easy way or reason to report what they find. Recovery depends on whoever happens to be holding the item, and the owner has no way to reach them.

Kittii is an AI-powered lost-and-found marketplace. Seekers report what they lost, a community of finders called Sherlocks report what they found, and AI matching pairs the two.

Why We Stopped Building and Started with 54 Interviews

Kittii began with friends from my post-graduate programme at Cape Breton University. The founder brought the idea to two of us, and it had already been put in front of Volta, the Atlantic Canada startup house, reaching the second round without getting through.

The feedback was that the idea was promising but the fundamentals were missing. That set the work for the next stage: do the discovery properly before building anything.

The target segment was frequent public transit commuters. For them, recovery today is informal and slow: it depends entirely on whoever happens to be holding the item, and the person who lost it has no way to reach them.

Every competitor ran short of finders

The platform is built for Seekers, but it only works because of Sherlocks.

A marketplace with two sides has a chicken-and-egg problem from day one. You cannot attract Seekers without Sherlocks reporting found items, and Sherlocks will not join a platform nobody has heard of.

Demand was never the problem.

Every lost-and-found platform I looked at failed on the same thing: too few people reporting what they had found.

Finders had no reason to report.

The database stayed empty and Seekers left disappointed.

Cash is not what moves finders.

61%of finder-motivation responses in our survey were altruism, and 31% were convenience. Only 6% were a cash reward.

Trust and privacy stop them.

Finders cited doubts about legitimacy (24%) and not wanting to reveal their identity (24%) as top reasons for not using an app.

Timing compounds it.

People who lose something need help inside a narrow panic window. Miss it and they have already given up.

So we flipped the model. Lead with Sherlocks, make reporting fast, verified and anonymous by default, and let that supply base pull Seekers in.

Building for the 0-to-1 MVP

#foundatkittii, a five-day treasure hunt

One question shaped the whole plan: how do we get 100 or more people to actively report found items, in five days, in one city, for almost nothing?

How it worked

  • Ten partner shops. Across Halifax's downtown peninsula, each with a branded standee at the payment counter.
  • One clue, one QR code. Every standee carried a unique clue tied to one item, linking to the submission form.
  • A $50 prize. The first matching report using a shop's clue won a gift card, redeemable only at that shop.
  • Paid search plus social. Search intercepted "lost item Halifax" queries at peak intent, while Reddit and Instagram carried the campaign locally.

It turned ten business owners into acquisition channels for $50 each. No sales team, no cold outreach, and the shop's own foot traffic doing the distribution.

A clear pass or fail, on purpose

The beta was capped at 200 to 250 submissions. A deliberate, controlled cohort rather than a push for scale.

Each number was set so the beta would give a clear yes or no, rather than numbers that merely looked encouraging. A 30 per cent recovery rate is the one that matters, because it is the only measure of whether the product delivers end-to-end value.

This plan was never executed. The team wound down before launch, so the campaign never ran and none of these targets were tested.

Reflection

Two things I would change, both visible only in hindsight.

  • We set a revenue target before we had a reliable way to bring in either side. A product with two sides is worth nothing until both sides are there at the same time, and the plan treated finders and seekers as two problems to solve one after the other, instead of one problem. The GTM eventually corrected for this, but it came late.
  • I would have tested the matching quality much earlier, against a small real corpus of lost and found reports. AI matching was the differentiator on paper, and it was the part with the least evidence behind it when the build started.

Where it stands. The build reached MVP and stopped there. The go-to-market plan was written but never run, and the revenue target was never tested. The product site stays up as a preview, though the product itself never launched.
